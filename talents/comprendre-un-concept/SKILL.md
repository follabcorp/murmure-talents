# Understand a concept

Make an idea understandable by starting from what the person already knows, then give a precise definition and an example. Suits technical, scientific, financial, or abstract notions.

## Process

1. Assess or ask the level: child, curious beginner, practitioner, or specialist. Adapt the vocabulary and the prerequisites. If nothing is specified, start from a curious person discovering the field, and offer to go further.
2. Build on knowledge the person already has and link it to the new idea: "you know how this mechanism works? Here is what it resembles."
3. Choose **one strong and accurate analogy**. Say what it explains and **where it stops working**, to avoid false pictures.
4. Give the precise definition after the intuition, then a concrete example, with numbers or a clearly described situation.
5. Organize the depth: one overall sentence, a short paragraph, then the detailed mechanisms. The person should be able to stop at the level they need.
6. Address explicitly a common confusion about the topic.
7. End with a short comprehension question, a task the person now knows how to do, or an invitation to go further.

## Adapting the explanation

| Audience | Form |
| --- | --- |
| Child | Everyday analogy, concrete objects, short sentences, simple vocabulary |
| Curious beginner | Analogy, simple definition, and an example; define each new term |
| Practitioner | Mechanisms, trade-offs, and use cases, with fewer analogies |
| Specialist | Nuances, limits, and precise terminology, without restating the basics |

## Worked example: a database index

**Idea:** like the index at the back of a book, it lets you reach a topic without reading every page.

**Mechanism:** without an index, a search may go through every row. An index keeps a search structure linking values to the matching rows, which reduces the number of elements to examine.

**Limit of the analogy:** a database index must also be updated during writes. It speeds up reads, but it takes up space and can slow down insertions.

**Question:** why avoid an index on information that is rarely searched but very often changed?

Define jargon the first time it is used, and favor numbers and situations. Avoid definitions that depend on other undefined terms, analogies without limits, a childish tone with a specialist, and details that overwhelm a beginner. Simplify without making it false: accuracy and a concrete example remain required. Check that the explanation has been understood rather than assuming it.

Reply in the language the person uses.
