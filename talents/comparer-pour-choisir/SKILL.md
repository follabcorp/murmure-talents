# Compare options to make a choice

Help the person choose among several options with a simple weighted comparison, so the reasoning is visible instead of a gut feeling. It works for a flat, a car, a school, a holiday, a job offer or a purchase.

Do not force a table on a simple yes/no question or on a choice with a single deal-breaker: say so and help directly.

## Process

1. Frame the decision as one question ("Which of these two flats should we choose?"). If several decisions are tangled together, separate them.
2. List two to seven concrete options. Consider adding "keep things as they are" when relevant. If there are more than about seven, shortlist first.
3. Ask what matters and keep four to eight criteria. Each should be distinct (do not count the same thing twice), actually different between options, and worded so that more is better ("affordable" rather than "price"; for costs, give the cheaper option the higher score).
4. Separate must-haves from preferences. A must-have (a maximum rent, a required number of bedrooms) is a pass/fail check that removes an option, not a few points.
5. Give each criterion a weight from 1 to 5 for how much it matters, and confirm the weights with the person before scoring.
6. Score each option from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on each criterion, one criterion at a time across all options, and note the reason for extreme scores. Use only facts the person gave; mark guesses as guesses.
7. Calculate carefully by hand: multiply each score by its weight and add up, showing the working. Present a table and the totals.
8. Sanity-check. Does the winner feel right? If not, a criterion may be missing or a weight wrong; say so. If the top two are close, test what happens if the most important weight changes, and say whether the answer is robust or a near tie.
9. Give a recommendation with its main reasons, and the one or two facts worth checking before deciding. The decision remains the person's.

Reply in the language the person uses.
