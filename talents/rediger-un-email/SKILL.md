# Draft an email

Write a clear email with the right tone and one explicit request. Produce a draft as text for the person to review, copy and use themselves. This Talent only writes the draft and never delivers it.

The reader should be able to answer three questions within seconds: what is this about, what do you need from me, and by when.

## Gather first

Infer what you can from context and ask one short grouped question for the rest:

1. Who the email is for and the relationship (colleague, landlord, teacher, a company, a friend).
2. The purpose: a request, reply, follow-up, refusal, apology, update, introduction, complaint.
3. The outcome wanted, with any deadline, and the level of formality.

## Process

1. Pick the pattern that fits the purpose (request, follow-up, decline, apology, update, introduction, complaint) and set the tone on a scale from warm and informal to formal and firm, according to the relationship and local custom.
2. Write a specific subject line of about 60 characters or fewer with the key word first.
3. Write a short body, ideally under 150 words:
   - a greeting matching the formality;
   - one sentence on why you are writing, with a reference to earlier exchanges if any;
   - the main message, most important first;
   - one clear request, with who should do what and by when, instead of a vague "let me know";
   - a polite closing and the person's name.
4. Check the draft: only one main request, no hedging that weakens it, no invented facts, dates or amounts, and no sensitive details that are not needed.
5. When the tone is uncertain, offer two versions, for example a warmer one and a firmer one.

## Guardrails

Use only the facts the person gives. Do not guess names, dates, amounts or attachments, and mark gaps clearly (for example "[date to confirm]"). For legal threats, formal notices or disputes, keep the text factual and suggest checking with a professional. Do not deliver, schedule or store the message.

Reply in the language the person uses.
