# Prepare a speech

Help prepare **and deliver** a talk: central message, narrative structure, opening, closing, spoken language, stage fright, and questions.

## Useful information

Gather the occasion, the audience, its size, and the level of formality; what the listeners should think, feel, or do next; the length; the format (slides, lectern, video call, or table); the ideas, stories, data, or drafts already available; and the worries (stage fright, overrunning, a difficult audience, questions).

## Process

1. Reduce the essential message to **one sentence** the audience should remember. Remove what does not serve that sentence.
2. Define the intended effect: inform, persuade, inspire, or entertain, with a concrete next step for the listeners.
3. Choose a suitable structure: problem then solution; a story; "What? Why does it matter? What now?"; or, for a toast, relationship, anecdote, and wish. One idea per part.
4. Write an opening that grabs attention (a story, a question, a verified fact, an image) and a closing that recalls the message. The first and last thirty seconds matter especially. To persuade, end with an explicit request.
5. Write for the ear: short sentences, concrete words, signposting, pauses, and stories. Read aloud and rework the passages that snag.
6. If slides are planned, use one idea per slide and useful images rather than walls of lists. Slides support the speech; they do not become the text to read out.
7. Plan a rehearsal **standing, aloud, and timed**, ideally recorded by the speaker. Aim for about 90% of the allotted time. Memorize the steps and transitions rather than a text word for word.
8. Prepare a warm-up and breathing, treat adrenaline as energy, and anticipate questions with short answers. Prepare an honest formula: "I don't know; I will check and come back to you."

## Guide by occasion

| Occasion | Structure and tone |
| --- | --- |
| Conference | Story or problem-solution; one big idea; engaging tone |
| Professional presentation | Conclusion first, then evidence; clarity and concision |
| Sales pitch | Problem, solution, proof, request; confidence |
| Toast or wedding | Relationship, short anecdote, sincere wish; warmth and brevity |
| Tribute | The person, one or two memories, what they leave behind; gentleness |
| Persuasive speech | Hook, stakes, argument, call to action; conviction |

A concrete anecdote sticks better than an abstraction. Silence and pauses can help the speaker find their voice. Avoid a hesitant opening, reading slides aloud in a monotone, rigid recitation, too many private jokes, and overrunning the time. For a toast or tribute, prepare rather than improvise a long speech.

Bodily and vocal practice belongs to the speaker: silent rereading does not replace it. In case of severe or disabling anxiety, suggest professional support alongside these techniques.

Reply in the language the person uses.
